Best Sunglasses for the Beach

Last reviewed: October 30, 2026. Sources for this page are listed in its fact sheet.

Short answer first. For Miami water and white sand, polarized lenses are the whole argument: they cut the glare that bounces off Biscayne Bay and the Atlantic, and once you have fished, boated or driven the causeways with them, plain lenses feel broken. Add full UV protection, a fit that survives sweat and sunscreen, and a strap or floating frame for anything involving a boat. Fashion frames are welcome everywhere else on this trip; on the water, function leads.

Polarized and UV: the two non negotiables

UV protection, labeled UV400 or 100 percent UVA and UVB, is the health feature and is independent of darkness or price. Polarization is the comfort feature, a filter that kills horizontal glare from water and wet sand. For drivers on the MacArthur Causeway, anglers, boaters and anyone reading a menu on a bright terrace, polarized is the default Miami choice. One caveat: polarized lenses can dim some boat instrument and phone screens at angles; glance, adjust, and it is a non issue for most users.

Lens colors for Miami light

Gray lenses keep colors true and suit the brightest beach days. Brown and copper raise contrast and flatter the bay's greens, popular with boaters and drivers. Mirrored coatings cut an extra slice of glare and add the South Beach look in one move. Whatever the tint, category darkness is about comfort; UV protection is the safety line, and cheap gas station pairs that state UV400 honestly beat dark lenses that do not. Fit, grip and the water insurance

Sunscreen makes every frame slippery: rubber nose pads and temple grips are the features that keep glasses on your face through a beach day. Sport wraps suit boat and bike days, classic frames suit the beach club, and both suit their photos. Retainer straps are mandatory equipment on boats and jet skis, and floating frames exist for the forgetful. Losing a pair overboard is a Miami rite of passage this paragraph exists to cancel; the lost and found of the bay keeps what it catches.

A two pair strategy

Regulars travel with the good polarized pair for the water and a cheap spare for the sand, the club and the crowd, where loss and scratches live. The spare also covers the friend who forgot theirs, a recurring character in every group trip including the bachelorette parties in our bachelorette guide. Pack the good pair in a hard case in your personal item, never loose in the beach bag's sandy depths.

Prescription wearers and kids

Two groups the standard advice skips. Prescription wearers have three honest routes: polarized prescription lenses in a frame they like, fit over shades that cover their glasses for water days, or contacts plus regular polarized pairs, which is what most boat going locals choose. Clip ons are the budget fourth and look it. For kids, the priorities reorder: a strap that keeps them on, flexible frames that survive being sat on, and the same UV400 labeling as adults, because small eyes take the same sun. Buy kids' pairs in twos; the bay accepts donations daily.

The facts that matter

Buying guide only: no brands, prices or ratings. UV claims should be verified on the product's own labeling (UV400 or 100 percent UVA/UVB); polarization is a glare filter and does not by itself imply UV protection. Nothing here substitutes for an eye professional's advice on prescription needs.

Frequently asked questions

Are polarized sunglasses worth it for the beach?
Yes, above all near water. The glare reduction is immediate and the eye fatigue difference by sunset is real.

Do darker lenses protect better?
No. Protection is the UV rating, not the tint. Dark lenses without UV protection are worse than none, since pupils open behind them.

What lens color for driving and boating?
Gray for truest color, copper or brown for contrast on the water. Both polarized in Miami light.

How do I stop losing sunglasses on boats?
A retainer strap every time, floating frames if you are a repeat offender, and the cheap spare for the riskiest hours.
