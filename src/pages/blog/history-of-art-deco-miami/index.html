How Art Deco Made Miami Beach

Last reviewed: October 30, 2026. Sources for this page are listed in its fact sheet.

Short answer first. Miami Beach's pastel skyline exists because three things collided: a 1920s land boom that needed cheap, cheerful buildings fast; a generation of architects who built tropical Deco hotels through the 1930s; and a 1970s preservation campaign, led by designer Barbara Baer Capitman, that stopped the bulldozers and got the district listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1979. This is the story in the order it happened.

Before Deco: the boom town

Miami Beach was incorporated in 1915, carved out of mangrove and sand on a barrier island. Developer Carl Fisher drained swamps, built causeways, and marketed the island as a winter playground, and the Florida land boom of the early 1920s filled in the first hotels and mansions in Mediterranean Revival style. The Great Miami Hurricane of September 1926 flattened much of the young city and burst the boom; the rebuilding that followed, into the teeth of the Depression, needed to be fast, modest, and optimistic. That is the economic recipe that produced Deco.

The 1930s wave: tropical Deco

Through the 1930s, architects including Henry Hohauser, L. Murray Dixon, and Albert Anis built hundreds of small hotels and apartment buildings along Ocean Drive, Collins Avenue, and Washington Avenue. The style they evolved is now called Tropical Deco or Streamline Moderne: white stucco, eyebrows over the windows, porthole cutouts, ship railings, neon signage, terrazzo floors, and nautical and tropical motifs, from flamingos to frozen fountains, meant to sell sunshine and modernity at Depression prices. The buildings were modest, most just three stories, but the repetition along the oceanfront made a streetscape unlike anywhere else in America. A second wave of grander resort hotels rose on the mid beach strip in the 1940s and 1950s, the Fontainebleau (1954) and Eden Roc (1956) among them, in the flamboyant postwar style locals call MiMo, Miami Modernism. Our guides to the Fontainebleau and Eden Roc pick the story up there.

The near loss, and the fight back

By the 1970s the Deco hotels were aging rooming houses and retirement residences, and demolition for parking lots and condos was steady. Designer and activist Barbara Baer Capitman founded the Miami Design Preservation League (MDPL) in 1976 and campaigned, building by building and hearing by hearing, for the district's protection. The result came on May 14, 1979, when the Miami Beach Architectural District, roughly a square mile of South Beach, was listed on the National Register of Historic Places, the first 20th century architectural district so recognized, per the MDPL. The listing did not freeze the buildings, but it changed the economics: restoration became the valuable move.

The pastel era

In the 1980s, designer Leonard Horowitz created the district's famous pastel color palette, painting the white buildings in soft ice cream tones that photographed as pure Miami. A 1985 run of the television series Miami Vice, all neon and pastel and Ocean Drive, beamed the look worldwide, and the fashion and film industries followed. Restoration money poured in; hotels like The Betsy (a Georgian Revival outlier from 1942, proof the district is not one style) returned as boutique properties. Today the district holds around 800 contributing historic structures, per the MDPL, the densest concentration of Art Deco architecture in the world, a claim the preservation league and the city both make and the building count supports.

How to read a Deco building

Stand on Ocean Drive and read in layers: the symmetrical facade with a raised central bay; the sign tower or fin with the hotel's name in neon; eyebrows, the concrete canopies over windows that shade the glass; portholes and nautical rails borrowed from ocean liners; and inside, a terrazzo lobby with a fountain or a relief panel. The Art Deco walking tour teaches the vocabulary on the street, and the Wolfsonian museum, covered in our museums guide, shows the era's design objects. The Art Deco Welcome Center at 1001 Ocean Drive, run by the MDPL, is the natural start and the source for the current tour schedule.

The facts that matter

Miami Beach incorporated 1915; the Great Miami Hurricane struck September 1926. MDPL founded 1976 by Barbara Baer Capitman. The Miami Beach Architectural District entered the National Register of Historic Places on May 14, 1979 (MDPL and National Park Service). Fontainebleau opened 1954, Eden Roc 1956. The district holds roughly 800 contributing historic structures per the MDPL, checked October 30, 2026.

Frequently asked questions

Where exactly is the Art Deco District?
Roughly the southern mile of Miami Beach, centered on Ocean Drive, Collins Avenue, and Washington Avenue from about 5th to 23rd streets.

Can you stay in a Deco hotel?
Yes. Many Ocean Drive and Collins Avenue properties are restored Deco buildings; our South Beach hotels guide flags the historic ones.

When is Art Deco Weekend?
The MDPL's festival runs each January on Ocean Drive; the current edition's dates are on the league's site and our events hub.
