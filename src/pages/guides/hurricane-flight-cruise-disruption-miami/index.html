Hurricane Disruption in Miami: Flights, Cruises, Rights

Last reviewed: October 30, 2026. Sources for this page are listed in its fact sheet.

Short answer first. Two different legal worlds govern your trip when a hurricane aims at Miami. Flights sit under US Department of Transportation rules that give you a refund when the airline cancels. Cruises sit under the cruise contract, which lets the line change or cancel for weather with no fare refund owed. Knowing which world each booking lives in, before the storm is named, is the difference between a disrupted trip and a disrupted trip that also costs you the money.

Flights: the DOT refund rule, stated exactly

The US Department of Transportation's rule is clean: if the airline cancels your flight, for any reason including weather, and you choose not to travel or do not accept credits or vouchers, you are entitled to a refund of the ticket price. The airline must tell you that you are entitled to a refund when it offers alternative transportation, refunds are automatic if you reject a rebooking or voucher offer, and they are due within 7 business days for credit card purchases and 20 calendar days for other payment forms.

The traps are behavioral, not legal. Accepting the voucher or the rebooking can close the refund door, so decide which you want before you tap accept in the app. Airline travel waivers, issued ahead of named storms, usually let you move dates without change fees inside a window, which is often the better tool if your trip can slide. And keep your booking in the airline's own channels; a refund conversation is always simpler with the seller who sold the ticket. Miami International Airport publishes live flight status on its own boards, and the airport's storm decisions, including any closure, are announced by the airport and the county at the time, never in advance by guides like this one.

Cruises: the contract rules, and it is not the DOT

Cruise lines are not under the flight refund rule, and the difference is not a technicality. Royal Caribbean's own FAQ, as the documented example, states the line may, without prior notice, cancel, advance, postpone, or deviate from any scheduled sailing or port of call for weather among other reasons, may substitute a ship or a port, and is not required to refund fare or compensate for those changes. Its hurricane guidance adds the operational reality: the line tries to avoid operating near a tropical system, alters itineraries to get around storms, and posts updates on its Travel Updates page.

Translated: a hurricane cruise from Miami usually sails, to different ports, on a possibly shifted schedule, with the fare intact. The money protections that actually exist are travel insurance with the right clauses, cruise line future cruise credit goodwill offers made case by case, and your card protections. Read your own line's contract and its travel updates page the moment a storm is named; PortMiami closure decisions are made by the port and the Coast Guard at the time and are announced then, not forecast by anyone.

The order to do things when a storm is named

One: move the flexible things first, the hotel dates and the rental car, while inventory exists. Two: decide flights on the waiver terms the airline publishes, and take the refund only if you are abandoning the trip. Three: read your cruise line's updates page and your contract before calling anyone; hold times in a named storm are measured in hours and the contract already contains most answers. Four: if you are already in Miami, follow Miami-Dade County and City of Miami Beach official channels for evacuation zones and orders, and treat the ocean as closed long before it looks dangerous; rip currents around storms kill more visitors than wind does.

For the planning side of all of this, when to book, what the season really looks like month by month, and how to build a refundable trip, read our hurricane season trip planning guide. Atlantic hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30 on the National Hurricane Center's climatology, with the peak around September 10 and most activity from mid August to mid October, an average season producing 14 named storms, 7 hurricanes, and 3 major hurricanes. Your odds on any given week are low; your odds across a whole season of Miami travel are why the refundable booking habit exists.

The facts that matter

Primary sources, checked October 30, 2026: US DOT: airline cancellation for any reason plus passenger choosing not to travel means a refund of the ticket price; airlines must tell you a refund is due when offering alternatives; refunds automatic if you reject rebooking or vouchers; due within 7 business days (credit card) or 20 calendar days (other payment). Cruise contracts differ: Royal Caribbean's FAQ states it may cancel, advance, postpone, or deviate any sailing or port for weather without prior notice, substitute ship or port, and is not required to refund fare or compensate. Frequently asked questions

My flight to Miami was cancelled for a hurricane. Do I get a refund?
Yes, if the airline cancels and you choose not to travel or reject credits and vouchers, US DOT rules entitle you to a refund of the ticket price, due within 7 business days on a card payment.

My cruise itinerary changed because of a storm. Do I get my money back?
Generally no. Cruise contracts let lines change ports and schedules for weather without a fare refund. Your protections are travel insurance, any goodwill credit the line offers, and card protections.

Should I accept the airline's voucher or rebooking?
Decide first: accepting a rebooking or voucher can end your refund entitlement. If the trip can slide, the airline's storm waiver usually lets you move dates without fees; if you are abandoning the trip, take the refund.

When is hurricane season in Miami?
June 1 to November 30, peaking around September 10, with most activity from mid August to mid October, per the National Hurricane Center.
