Miami Fishing Charters

Last reviewed: October 30, 2026. Sources for this page are listed in its fact sheet.

Short answer first. Miami fishing charters run from the marinas in Miami Beach, Bayside, and Haulover out to the Gulf Stream edge and the reef line, which sit unusually close here: boats can be fishing within a few miles of the beach. The two products are shared (split) charters, where you buy seats on a sportfishing boat with strangers at the lowest price per person, and private charters, where your group rents the boat for a half day (about 4 hours), three quarter day, or full day (about 8 hours). Your fare on a licensed charter boat includes the fishing licenses, tackle, and bait; the main extra is the mate's tip, customarily 15 to 20 percent. What you catch depends on season: sailfish and kingfish in the cool months, mahi mahi and tuna in the warm ones, snapper and grouper on the reefs through much of the year.

Shared versus private: the real difference

On a shared charter the boat takes up to about six anglers who did not know each other at breakfast. The crew sets the program, anglers rotate on strikes by a system the mate runs, and the price per person is the cheapest way onto a proper sportfisher in Miami. The compromises are real: no say in technique or target, and your fish might be caught by the person next to you. On a private charter the boat, the captain, and the plan are yours: kite fishing for sailfish in season, trolling for mahi, bottom fishing the wrecks for snapper, or a family trip that mixes fishing with a swim stop. Private costs several times the shared seat and is the right buy for serious anglers and groups of four to six, where the per person math narrows quickly.

What Miami fishing is actually like

The geography is the gift: the continental shelf drops off fast off Miami, so deep water and the northward Gulf Stream sit close to shore, and the run to the fishing grounds is short by Atlantic standards. Winter, roughly November through April, is sailfish season, when boats fly kites with live baits along the edge; it is also kingfish, wahoo, and the best bottom fishing weather between fronts. Late spring and summer belong to mahi mahi under weed lines and floating debris offshore, with blackfin tuna in the mix and the seas at their calmest. Reef trips for snapper and grouper run all year, subject to the species seasons and size limits the crew will enforce. Shark fishing and tarpon fishing in the passes and around the bridges are their own specialties; the bay also supports light tackle guides for bonefish and permit on the flats, a different and quieter sport sold by a different set of captains.

Costs, rules, and honest downsides

Beyond the fare: tip the mate 15 to 20 percent in cash, expect a fuel or premium bait conversation only if you agreed to specialty trips, and know that most boats will clean and bag your catch, with some marinas and restaurants happy to cook it for a fee. Florida fishing licenses are covered for passengers on licensed for hire vessels, so you do not need your own. The downsides are the ocean's: seasickness on choppy days, slow fishing on some days despite everyone's best work, and summer heat on an open cockpit. Captains cancel for unsafe seas, not for slow fishing, and no honest operator guarantees a catch. Book the trip early in your stay so a weather day can be moved, take the morning departure in summer, and bring sun protection you would wear in the desert; there is no shade that matters on a sportfisher at noon.

The facts that matter

Miami charters depart marinas in Miami Beach, downtown (Bayside), and Haulover, and fish the reef line and Gulf Stream edge a few miles offshore. Shared trips sell individual seats, usually to about six anglers; private trips rent the boat in half day (about 4 hours) and full day (about 8 hours) formats. Fares on licensed charter boats include fishing licenses, tackle, and bait. Sailfish peak in the cool months and mahi mahi in the warm months; species seasons and limits set by Florida and federal rules apply to the catch. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Florida fishing license?
No, not on a licensed charter boat. Passengers fishing from a for hire vessel operating under its own licenses are covered by the boat.

Shared or private charter for a first timer?
Shared for a solo or a couple testing the waters; private for groups, families with kids, or anyone with a specific target like sailfish.

What should I tip?
The mate works for tips; 15 to 20 percent of the charter price is customary, paid in cash, and adjusted for effort and conditions, not only for the catch.

Can we keep and eat the fish?
Legal sized fish in open seasons can be kept, and crews typically clean and bag the catch. Some waterfront restaurants will cook your catch; ask the marina when you return.
