Miami Beach Safety Guide

Last reviewed: October 30, 2026. Sources for this page are listed in its fact sheet.

Short answer first. Swim near a lifeguard, read the flags, and know the rip current rule before you need it: do not fight the current, float, swim parallel to shore to escape it, then angle back in, and wave for help if you cannot reach shore. That follows NOAA and National Weather Service guidance. Treat a purple flag as a marine life warning, leave at first thunder for 30 minutes past the last, and ask a lifeguard.

Before you swim: guards, flags, and the forecast

The safest Miami swim starts on the sand. The City of Miami Beach Ocean Rescue division advises swimming in a guarded area, near a lifeguard stand, because a lifeguard with an elevated view can see where rip currents are and direct swimmers away. NOAA gives the same advice in three rules: swim near a lifeguard, never swim alone, and if in doubt, do not go out. Note where that cannot be followed: Florida State Parks states that Bill Baggs has no lifeguards, so swimming there is at your own risk. Before you travel, check the National Weather Service rip current outlook, then check the flags and signs again at the stand.

Rip currents: what to do if one catches you

NOAA describes rip currents as powerful, channeled currents flowing away from shore that can occur at any beach with breaking waves. They pull you away from shore; they do not pull you under. The NOAA and National Weather Service guidance is consistent, and this page follows it. Do not fight the current and do not swim directly back to shore against it, because that tires swimmers out. Relax and float to conserve energy. Swim parallel to the shoreline until you are out of the pull, then swim at an angle away from the current and toward shore, letting waves help push you in. If you cannot reach shore, float or tread water, face the beach, and yell and wave for help. If you see someone else in trouble, do not enter the water yourself: alert a lifeguard or call 911, and throw something that floats if you can do so safely.

The warning flags: what each color means

Florida uses a uniform warning flag system, described by the Florida Department of Environmental Protection and used by lifeguards in Miami. Green means low hazard and calm conditions, with caution still advised, because low hazard is not no hazard. Yellow means medium hazard, with moderate surf and or currents, so proceed with care. A single red flag means high hazard, with high surf and or strong currents: avoid entering the water. A double red flag means the water is closed to the public. Purple means dangerous marine life is present, such as jellyfish, man o' war, or sea lice, and it may fly together with another flag, because it answers a different question from the surf colors. The flags in front of you outrank any guide. No flags does not by itself assure safe water, so look for signs and ask a lifeguard.

Jellyfish and Portuguese man o' war

A purple flag is your first warning, and the sand is your second: Portuguese man o' war and jellyfish can wash ashore, and official guidance warns not to touch them, including specimens that look dead, because tentacles can still sting. Florida State Parks posts winter man o' war warnings at the Bill Baggs ranger station. If you are stung, leave the water and go to the nearest lifeguard for help first. Florida's poison centers advise calling the Florida Poison Information Center Network at 1-800-222-1222 for treatment instructions, and calling 911 if there is trouble breathing, faintness, or chest pain. Do not remove tentacles with bare fingers, and do not rely on folk remedies. Follow the lifeguard's and the poison center's instructions for the sting in front of you, because the right first aid can differ by species.

Lightning: when thunder roars, go indoors

Afternoon storms are normal in Miami, and the National Weather Service rule is simple: when thunder roars, go indoors. If you can hear thunder, you are within striking distance, so get out of the water and off the beach at the first thunder, and go to a large enclosed building or a fully enclosed metal vehicle. Beach shacks, picnic shelters, and tents are not safe shelter. Wait until 30 minutes after the last clap of thunder before returning outside. Lightning can strike as far as 10 miles from where rain is falling, per the Weather Service, so a blue patch overhead is not an all clear while a storm is still audible.

A short routine to use at any Miami beach

Use a fixed order. Read the flags first. Sit near a lifeguard stand where guards are provided, and ask where currents are running. Agree nobody swims alone, and re check when flags change. Treat a purple flag, a washed up man o' war, or thunder as a reason to pause. At an unguarded beach, swim less far out and keep children within arm's reach.

The facts that matter

Rip currents: do not fight it, float, swim parallel to escape, then angle to shore; if you cannot, float and wave for help (NOAA). Flags (Florida Department of Environmental Protection): green low, yellow medium, red high, double red closed, purple dangerous marine life. Stings: leave the water, go to a lifeguard, call Florida Poison Information at 1-800-222-1222, or 911 for trouble breathing. Lightning: go indoors, wait 30 minutes after the last thunder (National Weather Service).

Frequently asked questions

What is the most important rule?
Swim near a lifeguard. The city and NOAA both start there, because a guard can see rip currents and post the flags.

Does a green flag mean the water is safe?
No. Green means low hazard, with caution still advised. The absence of flags does not assure safe water.

What does a purple flag mean?
Dangerous marine life is present, such as jellyfish or man o' war. It can fly alongside another flag.

How long should we stay off the beach in a thunderstorm?
Until 30 minutes after the last thunder, in a large enclosed building or a fully enclosed vehicle, per the National Weather Service.
