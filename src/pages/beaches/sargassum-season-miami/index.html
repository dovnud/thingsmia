Sargassum Season in Miami: What Visitors Need to Know

Last reviewed: October 30, 2026. Sources for this page are listed in its fact sheet.

Short answer first. Sargassum is a natural, free floating brown seaweed. In Miami Beach, the city says large amounts typically arrive from March through October, varying day by day with winds, currents, and tides. County crews work daily to remove buildup at the water line. No outlook can promise a clear beach on a specific date, because outlooks forecast risk at sea, not one stretch of sand. Check conditions close to your trip and keep a bay side or pool backup.

What sargassum actually is

NOAA defines sargassum as a type of floating brown algae, commonly called seaweed, that floats at the surface, never attaches to the sea floor, and can aggregate into large mats. Small gas filled structures, called pneumatocysts, give it buoyancy. Out at sea, NOAA describes those rafts as food, refuge, and breeding grounds for fish, sea turtles, marine birds, crabs, and shrimp, and as a nursery area for fish such as mahi mahi, jacks, and amberjacks. Historically, most gathered in the Sargasso Sea. Since 2011, NOAA says, its range has expanded, and a population in the tropical Atlantic, called the Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt, has sent larger amounts west toward the Caribbean, the Gulf, and Florida.

When it tends to reach Miami, and why the timing is a tendency

The City of Miami Beach states the local pattern directly: since 2011 the amount reaching Caribbean and Florida coastlines has increased, and in Miami Beach large amounts typically arrive from March through October. Miami-Dade County adds that South Florida has experienced high levels in recent years. Read that as a tendency, roughly spring through fall, not a calendar promise. A light week can fall in July, and a heavy landing can fall outside it, because delivery depends on local wind, currents, and tides. Travelling outside the typical window lowers the odds on the city's pattern, but it does not remove them.

What it is like on the beach, stated carefully

On shore, sargassum collects at the water line and decomposes in the sun. The City of Miami Beach says decomposition can produce odors from natural hydrogen sulfide and ammonia gases, the rotten egg smell visitors describe. The Florida Department of Health gives the health picture in measured terms, and this page follows it. The department says sargassum is not a toxin producing algae, that hydrogen sulfide can irritate the eyes, nose, and throat, and that people with asthma or other breathing conditions may be more sensitive. It also says sargassum itself does not sting or cause rashes, while tiny organisms living in it, such as jellyfish larvae, may irritate skin on contact, so it advises avoiding touching or swimming near the seaweed. In open beach air, it says the general public should not expect adverse health impacts from the gas.

How Miami-Dade County cleans it up

Cleanup is daily, not a one time fix. Miami-Dade County Parks says its crews operate seven days a week, picking up litter and cutting and turning seaweed into the sand at the water line, and operate daily to remove buildup there. The City of Miami Beach adds that sargassum is removed from hot spot areas and cut into the shoreline daily by the county through a state permit. Two limits follow. A fresh landing after the morning pass can change a beach by afternoon, and cleanup manages the beach, not the ocean, so a heavy day can outpace good work. Judge the beach in front of you that morning.

What forecasts can and cannot tell you

NOAA and the University of South Florida produce experimental inundation reports and satellite tracking that show where sargassum is at sea and the risk of it washing ashore in the Caribbean, the Gulf, and southeast Florida. Use them as a regional risk outlook, useful weeks ahead for expectations and days ahead for choosing a coast. They are not a promise for one beach on one morning. The city makes the limit explicit: satellites can track sargassum at sea, but the exact amount and location landing daily varies with local winds, currents, and tides. Conditions differ beach by beach on the same day. Check the outlook before you book, then check local conditions in the last few days.

How to plan a trip around it

Build flexibility in first. Do not depend on one beach, and know your alternatives: a bay side day at Matheson Hammock, a boat day from the tours section, or a city day from the guides section. Walk the shoreline early and move if the water line is stacked. Swim away from dense mats and rinse after contact. Many days in the window are ordinary beach days. The aim is a plan that survives a bad day.

The facts that matter

Sargassum is a floating brown algae that forms habitat mats at sea (NOAA). The Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt has expanded since 2011 (NOAA). In Miami Beach, large amounts typically arrive March through October, varying daily with winds, currents, and tides (City of Miami Beach). Decomposing sargassum can produce a rotten egg odor; the Florida Department of Health says it does not sting, while organisms living in it may irritate skin. County crews clean daily. Forecasts are risk outlooks, not guarantees.

Frequently asked questions

Can I still swim when there is sargassum?
Often, yes, if the entry is clear and no other warning applies. Florida health guidance advises avoiding touching or swimming near the seaweed itself, because organisms living in it may irritate skin. If mats are dense, choose a clearer stretch.

Will sargassum ruin a trip in June or August?
Nobody can promise that, in either direction. Those months sit inside the typical March to October window, so the risk is real, but landings vary daily and crews clean daily. Plan flexibly rather than cancelling on a forecast.

Is sargassum dangerous?
Per the Florida Department of Health, it is not a toxin producing algae. Its gas can irritate eyes, nose, and throat.

Where can I check?
Use the NOAA and University of South Florida outlooks for regional risk, then check local conditions close to the day.
